Project Overview

RuralTech Bridge is an educational technology initiative I founded to explore practical approaches to improving technology education and digital inclusion in rural and underserved communities in Ghana.

The initiative focuses on an important challenge: students are expected to develop computing and digital skills, yet some schools have limited access to computers, reliable electricity, internet connectivity, and other technology infrastructure.

Rather than waiting for every school to have a fully equipped computer laboratory, RuralTech Bridge explores learning experiences that can introduce foundational computing concepts using affordable, accessible, and locally appropriate educational materials.

The Problem

Computer and digital skills are increasingly important for education, employment, communication, and participation in a technology-driven society.

However, teaching computing can be challenging in schools where students have limited opportunities to interact directly with computers and where electricity or internet connectivity may be unreliable or unavailable.

This creates an educational access gap: students may be expected to learn about computers and digital technologies without having consistent access to the technologies they are studying.

Target Users

  • Upper-primary students in rural and underserved schools.
  • Teachers delivering computing-related lessons with limited technology resources.
  • Schools with limited access to computers, electricity, or reliable internet connectivity.
  • Educational organizations interested in low-resource and inclusive technology education.

Current Product

Computer Science Without Computers

The current RuralTech Bridge product concept is Computer Science Without Computers, an offline learning kit designed to help students learn foundational computing concepts through hands-on activities without requiring continuous access to a computer.

The approach translates computing concepts into physical, collaborative, and classroom-friendly activities that can be used in low-resource learning environments.

The goal is not to replace access to computers. Instead, the learning kit is intended to provide a practical bridge that allows students to begin developing computing knowledge while schools continue working toward greater access to digital infrastructure.

Current Learning Materials

The developing learning-kit concept includes physical and teacher-supported materials such as:

  • Picture cards representing computer components and digital technologies.
  • Name cards that help students identify computing devices and components.
  • Function cards explaining what different computer components do.
  • Matching activities connecting computer components with their names and functions.
  • Challenge cards that encourage students to apply their understanding through classroom activities.
  • Learning mats for organizing collaborative and hands-on activities.
  • Teacher-support materials explaining how to facilitate activities using the learning kit.

How the Product Works

Students interact with physical learning materials to identify, classify, match, organize, and explain computing concepts. Activities are designed to encourage participation and discussion rather than relying only on textbook descriptions.

For example, students can match an image of a computer component with its name and function, organize components according to their roles, and complete challenge activities that require them to explain how different parts of a computer system work together.

This approach makes abstract computing concepts more tangible while supporting collaborative learning in classrooms where access to digital devices may be limited.

Needs Assessment

Early development of the project included an initial needs survey with 13 respondents to better understand technology access, teaching challenges, and opportunities for supporting computing education in resource-constrained schools.

The needs-assessment process helped refine the problem definition, target users, value proposition, and direction of the initial learning-kit concept.

The project continues to use feedback and iterative development to ensure that future prototypes respond to the realities of the schools and educators the initiative is intended to support.

Human-Centered Design Approach

RuralTech Bridge applies a human-centered approach by beginning with the needs, constraints, and learning environments of the people who will use the product.

Product decisions consider factors such as infrastructure limitations, affordability, classroom usability, teacher facilitation, student participation, and the ability to use learning materials without continuous electricity or internet connectivity.

The project is being developed iteratively so that feedback from educators, students, mentors, and other stakeholders can inform future versions of the product.

Product Development Process

1. Problem Identification

Identified the challenge of teaching computing concepts in schools with limited access to computers, electricity, and internet connectivity.

2. Needs Assessment

Gathered initial feedback to better understand technology access, classroom challenges, and potential user needs.

3. Product Concept

Developed the Computer Science Without Computers concept as an offline and hands-on approach to foundational computing education.

4. Prototype Development

Began developing learning cards, activity materials, classroom challenges, learning mats, and teacher-support resources.

5. Future Testing

Future development will include prototype evaluation with educators and students, refinement of learning activities, and school-based pilot testing.

Project Impact

RuralTech Bridge explores how educational technology can be designed around the realities of communities where access to digital infrastructure remains limited.

Computer Science Without Computers aims to reduce the barrier between students and foundational computing education by creating learning experiences that can begin with the resources already available in the classroom.

The longer-term goal is to contribute to more inclusive pathways into computing and digital learning for students in rural and underserved communities.
